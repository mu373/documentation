# Maximum-Entropy Ensembles of Graphs

## Graph Ensembles

### State Space

A graph ensemble is a probability distribution $P(G)$ over a set of graphs, where each graph is a microstate. This statistical-mechanics view of networks is the starting point for maximum-entropy network models [[1]](#references)[[2]](#references).

If we think of a graph ensemble consisted of 3 nodes, there are three possible undirected edges and therefore $2^3=8$ graphs in the ensemble.

### Graph Observables

Observables are functions of a graph. Examples include edge count $L(G)$, node degrees $k_i(G)$, triangle count, clustering, or any other statistic we may want an ensemble to preserve.

### Probability Distributions on Graphs

The probability distributions on the graph must satisfy

$$
\sum_G P(G)=1.
$$

If normalization is the only constraint, the maximum-entropy distribution is **uniform over all graphs**. For $N=3$, that means $P(G)=1/8$ and the entropy is $\log_2 8=3$ bits.

## Maximum Entropy

### Unconstrained Maximum Entropy

Without constraints other than normalization, the entropy-maximizing distribution is uniform. This is the global maximum over the whole probability simplex.

For the eight graphs on three nodes,

$$
P(G)=\frac{1}{8},\qquad S=\log_2 8=3.
$$

### Constrained Maximum Entropy

The maximum-entropy principle says to preserve only the facts we choose as constraints and otherwise use the distribution with the largest entropy [[5]](#references). For graph ensembles, those facts are often expected observables $\langle x_a\rangle$ such as the expected total edge count and the degree distribution:

$$
\langle x_a\rangle=\sum_G P(G)\,x_a(G)=x_a^{*}.
$$

Among all graph distributions satisfying those equations, choose the one with largest Shannon entropy:

$$
S=-\sum_G P(G)\ln P(G).
$$

The result is the least-committed distribution consistent with the constraints.

### Lagrange Multipliers

We maximize entropy over the probabilities of all graphs: $P(G_1),P(G_2),\ldots$. The constraints are equations that those probabilities must satisfy:


$$
\sum_G P(G)=1,\qquad \sum_G P(G)x_a(G)=x_a^*\quad\text{for each observable }a.
$$


The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ combines the entropy objective with terms enforcing normalization and the observable constraints:


$$
\mathcal{L}=
\underbrace{-\sum_G P(G)\ln P(G)}_{\text{entropy}}
-\underbrace{\alpha\left(\sum_G P(G)-1\right)}_{\text{normalization}}
-\underbrace{\sum_a\lambda_a\left(\sum_G P(G)x_a(G)-x_a^*\right)}_{\text{observable constraints}}.
$$


At the maximum, changing one graph's probability while holding the others fixed should not improve the constrained objective. Thus $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P(G)=0$ gives

$$
-\ln P(G)-1-\alpha-\sum_a\lambda_a x_a(G)=0.
$$


Solving this equation for $P(G)$ gives
$$P(G)\propto e^{-\sum_a\lambda_a x_a(G)}$$
.
The normalization constant is the partition function $Z$.

Lagrange multipliers turn the constrained optimization into the Gibbs-Boltzmann form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(G)&=\frac{e^{-H(G)}}{Z},\\
H(G)&=\sum_a \lambda_a\,x_a(G),\\
Z&=\sum_G e^{-H(G)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The multipliers are fixed by the constraint equations $\langle x_a\rangle=-\partial\ln Z/\partial\lambda_a=x_a^{*}$. In this page, the multipliers are best understood as fields conjugate to graph observables.

## Canonical Ensembles

Canonical ensembles use soft constraints: observables are **fixed in expectation**, not exactly graph by graph.

### Example: Expected Edge Count

Here, we use expected edge count as the running example of a canonical ensemble. The constrained observable is the number of edges, and the constraint is soft:

$$
\langle L\rangle=\ell.
$$

The Hamiltonian is

$$
H(G)=\lambda L(G).
$$

An ensemble tuned to $\langle L\rangle$ still assigns probability to graphs with several possible realized edge counts. Only the average is pinned.

#### Partition Function

Because $L(G)=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}$, the partition function factorizes over possible edges:

$$
Z(\lambda)=\sum_G e^{-\lambda L(G)}=(1+e^{-\lambda})^M,
$$

where $M=\binom{N}{2}$ is the number of possible undirected edges.

<details className="derivation-details">
<summary>Step-by-step</summary>

Write a graph $G$ by its adjacency matrix. Let $a_{ij}$ be the $(i,j)$ entry:

$$
a_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if edge }(i,j)\text{ is present},\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

For a simple undirected graph on $N$ labeled nodes, there are

$$
M=\binom{N}{2}
$$

possible edges. The edge count is the sum of these indicators:

$$
L(G)=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}.
$$

With the edge-count Hamiltonian $H(G)=\lambda L(G)$, the partition function is

$$
Z(\lambda)
=\sum_G e^{-\lambda L(G)}
=\sum_{\{a_{ij}\}}
\exp\!\left(-\lambda\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}\right).
$$

The exponential of a sum becomes a product:

$$
\exp\!\left(-\lambda\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}\right)
=\prod_{i<j}e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}.
$$

Therefore the sum over all graphs is a sum over all binary choices for all possible edges:

$$
Z(\lambda)
=\sum_{a_{12}=0}^{1}\sum_{a_{13}=0}^{1}\cdots
\sum_{a_{N-1,N}=0}^{1}
\prod_{i<j}e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}.
$$

Because the product separates by edge, the sums factorize:

$$
Z(\lambda)
=\prod_{i<j}\sum_{a_{ij}=0}^{1}e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}.
$$

Each one-edge sum has only two terms:

$$
\sum_{a_{ij}=0}^{1}e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}
=e^{-\lambda\cdot 0}+e^{-\lambda\cdot 1}
=1+e^{-\lambda}.
$$

Since the same factor appears once for each of the $M$ possible edges,

$$
Z(\lambda)=\sum_G e^{-\lambda L(G)}=(1+e^{-\lambda})^M,
$$

</details>

#### Erdos-Renyi G(N,p)

The factorization means every possible edge is an independent Bernoulli trial:

$$
a_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p),
\qquad
p=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{1+e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

Equivalently, a graph with $L(G)$ present edges has probability

$$
P(G)=p^{L(G)}(1-p)^{M-L(G)}.
$$

<details className="derivation-details">
<summary>Step-by-step</summary>

Now substitute the factorized partition function back into the Gibbs distribution:

$$
P(G)=\frac{e^{-\lambda L(G)}}{Z(\lambda)}.
$$

Using $L(G)=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}$ and $Z(\lambda)=(1+e^{-\lambda})^M$,

$$
P(G)
=\frac{\prod_{i<j}e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}}
{\prod_{i<j}(1+e^{-\lambda})}
=\prod_{i<j}
\frac{e^{-\lambda a_{ij}}}{1+e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

This shows that the probability of a whole graph is a product of one-edge probabilities. For a single edge,

$$
P(a_{ij}=0)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda}},
\qquad
P(a_{ij}=1)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{1+e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

Define

$$
p=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{1+e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

Then

$$
1-p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda}},
$$

so each edge probability can be written as

$$
P(a_{ij})=p^{a_{ij}}(1-p)^{1-a_{ij}}.
$$

Thus every possible edge is an independent Bernoulli trial:

$$
a_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p),
\qquad
p=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{1+e^{-\lambda}}.
$$

For a graph with $L(G)$ present edges and $M-L(G)$ absent edges, the graph probability becomes

$$
P(G)
=\prod_{i<j}p^{a_{ij}}(1-p)^{1-a_{ij}}
=p^{L(G)}(1-p)^{M-L(G)}.
$$

This is exactly the Gilbert random graph model $G(N,p)$: each of the $M$ possible edges is included independently with probability $p$.

</details>

The maximum-entropy calculation determines the exponential form of the distribution, but the constraint value determines the numerical value of $\lambda$. For the edge-count ensemble, $\langle L\rangle=Mp$ with $M=\binom{N}{2}$, so a target $\ell$ implies $p=\ell/M$ and therefore

$$
\lambda=\log\frac{1-p}{p}
=\log\frac{M-\ell}{\ell}.
$$

Thus $\lambda$ is the log-odds against an edge: positive values make sparse graphs more likely, negative values make dense graphs more likely, and $\lambda=0$ gives the uniform ensemble.

This is Gilbert's $G(N,p)$ model: every possible edge is present independently with the same probability $p$ [[3]](#references). For $N=3$, there are three trials, so $\langle L\rangle=3p$ and $\langle k\rangle=2p$.

#### Edge-Count Distribution

A particular graph with $L$ present edges has probability

$$
P(G)=p^L(1-p)^{3-L}.
$$

Collapsing the eight graph probabilities by edge count gives a binomial distribution:

$$
P(L)=\binom{3}{L}p^{L}(1-p)^{3-L}.
$$

## Microcanonical Ensembles

Microcanonical ensembles use hard constraints: an observable is fixed exactly on every graph in the support. Again, **microcanonical** says how the constraint is enforced; the observable says what is fixed.

### Exact Edge Count

For edge count, the hard constraint is

$$
L(G)=m.
$$

This gives the fixed-edge model $G(N,m)$, the original Erdos-Renyi random graph model [[4]](#references).

### Uniform Distribution on a Constraint Set

The maximum-entropy solution is uniform over all graphs satisfying the hard constraint:

$$
P(G)=
\begin{cases}
1/|\{G:L(G)=m\}| & \text{if } L(G)=m,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

For $N=3$ and $m=1$, the constraint set has three graphs, so the entropy is $\log_2 3\approx 1.59$ bits.

### Canonical vs Microcanonical

A chosen constraint value gives one entropy-maximizing distribution. Varying the constraint value gives a family of maximum-entropy distributions.

For one global edge-count constraint, the canonical model $G(N,p)$ and microcanonical model $G(N,m)$ become equivalent as $N\to\infty$ when $m$ and $p$ are matched by edge density. For small $N$, they are visibly different: a canonical model tuned to $\langle L\rangle=1$ still leaves probability on $L=0,2,3$, while the microcanonical model puts all mass on $L=1$.

## Degree-Constrained Ensembles

The same maximum-entropy framework can constrain node degrees instead of only the total edge count. That gives the configuration-model family: the soft configuration model fixes expected degrees, while the hard configuration model fixes the degree sequence exactly. See [Configuration model](./configuration-model/) for that version.

## Ensemble Equivalence

### Global Constraints

Equivalence is most natural for a small number of global, self-averaging constraints. Edge count is the basic example. In $G(N,p)$, the realized edge count $L$ fluctuates around $Mp$. Equivalently, the realized edge density $\hat p=L/M$ fluctuates around $p$, but those fluctuations shrink as $N$ grows because $M=\binom{N}{2}$ grows.

### Local Constraints

Degree-sequence constraints are local and extensive: there is one constraint per node. The canonical ensemble allows each degree to fluctuate around its target; the microcanonical ensemble forbids those fluctuations exactly.

### Canonical-Microcanonical Non-Equivalence

Canonical and microcanonical ensembles can fail to become equivalent when the number of constraints grows with the system. Graph ensembles with a fixed number of edges are equivalent in the large-graph limit, while ensembles with a fixed degree sequence need not be [[7]](#references).

This is why $G(N,p)$ and $G(N,m)$ are usually interchangeable asymptotically, while soft and hard configuration models can remain measurably different.

## Interactive Example for N=3

The smallest nontrivial case is $N=3$. There are three possible undirected edges, so the full graph space has only $2^3=8$ graphs. That makes it possible to show the entire probability distribution $P(G)$ at once.

Use the tabs to compare three ways of assigning probabilities to the same eight graphs. The edge-count mode is the canonical $G(N,p)$ ensemble: one expected edge count $\langle L\rangle$ fixes one probability $p$ shared by all edges. The degree mode replaces that global constraint with three local expected-degree constraints. Free play lets you draw an arbitrary distribution, so you can see what is lost when the distribution no longer has the maximum-entropy form.

<MaxentGraphExample />

### Probability Distribution of Graphs

The first chart shows the probability of each individual graph. The graphs are grouped by their realized edge count $L=0,1,2,3$. In edge-count mode, all graphs with the same $L$ have the same probability because the Hamiltonian only contains $L(G)$.

### Edge Count Distribution
The second chart collapses those eight microstates onto the observable $L$. This is where degeneracy appears: the levels $L=0,1,2,3$ contain $1,3,3,1$ graphs. The control pins the expectation $\langle L\rangle$, not the realized value of $L$, so the observed edge count still fluctuates.

For the edge-count ensemble, those fluctuations follow the binomial law for three Bernoulli trials:

$$
P(L)=\binom{3}{L}p^{L}(1-p)^{3-L}.
$$

### Entropy Ceiling

The third chart compares entropy against the maximum possible entropy at the same expected edge count. The blue curve is

$$
S_{\max}(\langle L\rangle)=3\,h\!\left(\langle L\rangle/3\right),
$$

where $h$ is the binary entropy in bits. This is the constrained maximum-entropy envelope: for each value of $\langle L\rangle$, it gives the entropy of the canonical distribution with that expected edge count.

The binary entropy function is

$$
h(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p).
$$

For example, when $\langle L\rangle=1.5$,

$$
p=\frac{\langle L\rangle}{3}=\frac{1.5}{3}=0.5,
\qquad
h(0.5)=1,
$$

so

$$
S_{\max}(1.5)=3h(0.5)=3\ \text{bits}.
$$

This is the peak of the envelope: $p=1/2$, every graph has probability $1/8$, and the entropy reaches the unconstrained global maximum $\log_2 8=3$ bits.

Any extra structure beyond the mean edge count lowers entropy. In the node-degree mode, fixing three expected degrees also fixes $\langle L\rangle$, but it adds node-specific information. The coral gap is the entropy spent to buy that extra structure. In free play, the same gap appears whenever the hand-drawn distribution has more structure than the maximum-entropy distribution at the same $\langle L\rangle$.
